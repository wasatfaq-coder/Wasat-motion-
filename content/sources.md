## S1. Wittmann & Lehnhoff, "Age effects in perception of time", Psychological Reports 97 (2005), 921–935. PubMed 16512313
Also covered by Scientific American, "Why Does Time Fly as We Get Older?"

- S1.1 Survey of 499 participants aged 14 to 94.
- S1.2 For short intervals (the last hour, week, month) the felt speed of time did not increase with age.
- S1.3 For the question "How fast did the last 10 years pass for you?" answers tended to increase with age, peaking at about age 50 and then staying steady until the mid-90s.
- S1.4 Most respondents of all ages said time passes quickly.
- S1.5 Age effects were small to moderate and explained at most 10 % of the variance.

### S1 direct check (2026-11-12)
- PubMed abstract (PMID 16512313, Psychol Rep 2005;97(3):921-35, doi 10.2466/pr0.97.3.921-935): "subjective time perception by 499 subjects, ages 14 to 94 years ... the momentary perception of the passage of time and the retrospective judgment of past periods of time are a function of chronological age; however, small-to-moderate effects accounted for at most 10% of the variance ... interpretations have to be treated with caution given methodological limitations."
- Scientific American, Jordan Gaines Lewis, "Why Does Time Fly as We Get Older?", 2013-12-18: "Wittman and Lehnhoff found a weak association between age and the individuals' perception of time; in other words, everybody, regardless of age, thought that time was passing quickly. The question, 'How fast did the last 10 years pass for you?' yielded a tendency ... to increase with age; this pattern peaked at age 50, however, and remained steady until the mid-90s. Questions regarding smaller intervals of time ('How fast did the last hour/week/month pass?') did not change with age."
- Same article: Friedman & Janssen (2010) replicated that both age groups perceived short periods (hours, weeks, months) similarly, while older adults reported the last 10 years passed more quickly.
- Nuance: the abstract also reports a small age effect on momentary perception; the video's claim is limited to the short-interval questions (hour/week/month) as reported by Scientific American, and says the effect is small (S1.5).
- Correction to S1.2: the article lists hour/week/month; "even a year" came from a search summary and is NOT confirmed. Do not say "год" for the short-interval result.

## S2. Norman Bradburn, clarity-of-memory hypothesis, 1987 (via Scientific American)
- S2.1 People judge how long ago something happened by how vivid the memory feels: faded memories seem older than they are.

## S3. Claudia Hammond, "Time Warped" (2012): the holiday paradox
- S3.1 A holiday seems to pass quickly while you are in it, but looking back it feels like you were away for ages.
- S3.2 We judge time in two ways: prospectively (while it passes) and retrospectively (looking back).
- S3.3 Retrospective judgment of a period depends on how many new memories were formed in it; new sights and experiences add many memories.
- S3.4 Advice reported from Hammond: seek variety and new experiences; pause to notice.

## S4. Routine and temporal landmarks (popular coverage, 2020s)
- S4.1 Fewer new experiences in adult life mean fewer distinct memories, so recent years feel shorter in hindsight (hypothesis).
- S4.2 A 2025 review cautions that novelty does not always lengthen remembered time as predicted; attention and emotion also matter. Novelty is a hypothesis under dispute, not a proven reset.

## S5. Paul Janet, proportional ("time alive") idea, 19th century (via Noema / 3 Quarks Daily)
- S5.1 A year at age 10 is 10 % of the whole life lived; at 50 it is about 2 %.
- S5.2 Exact year of publication not confirmed (1877 vs 1890s): do not state the year.

## S6. Adrian Bejan, "Why the Days Seem Shorter as We Get Older", European Review 27(2), 2019, 187–194, doi 10.1017/S1062798718000741
- S6.1 Perceived time tracks the change of mental images; a young mind perceives more images per day than the same mind in old age.
- S6.2 Specific saccade-per-second figures in press coverage are unverified: do not use them.

## S7. William James, Principles of Psychology (1890), via Scientific American 2013
- S7.1 "we may be measuring past intervals of time by the number of events that can be recalled in that period" (hypothesis, attributed to James).
- S7.2 James: fewer memorable events in adulthood make "the years grow hollow and collapse".
